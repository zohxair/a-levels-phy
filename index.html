    Errors and uncertainties

    Systematic error
    Makes every reading too high or too low by the same amount or proportion, such as a zero error on a meter or a stretched tape. Repeating readings does not reduce it; it is removed by correcting the instrument or the method.
    Random error
    Makes readings scatter unpredictably either side of the true value, such as reaction time when using a stopwatch. Reduced by repeating readings and averaging.
    Accuracy
    How close a measurement is to the true value. Affected by systematic error.
    Precision
    How close repeated measurements are to each other (small scatter). Affected by random error.

    If a length is x = 25.0 ± 0.5 cm, the absolute uncertainty is 0.5 cm, the fractional uncertainty is 0.5/25.0 = 0.02, and the percentage uncertainty is 2%.

    CalculationRule
    Adding or subtracting, y = a ± bAdd the absolute uncertainties: Δy = Δa + Δb
    Multiplying or dividing, y = ab or a/bAdd the fractional (or percentage) uncertainties: Δyy = Δaa + Δbb
    Powers, y = anMultiply the fractional uncertainty by the power: Δyy = nΔaa

    Worked example

    A block has mass m = 25.0 ± 0.1 g and volume V = 10.0 ± 0.2 cm3. Find its density with its uncertainty.

    1. ρ = m/V = 25.0/10.0 = 2.50 g cm−3
    2. Percentage uncertainties: m: 0.4%, V: 2.0%. Total = 2.4%.
    3. 2.4% of 2.50 = 0.06 g cm−3

    ρ = 2.50 ± 0.06 g cm−3

    Exam tipGive the uncertainty to one (or at most two) significant figures, and round the value to the same decimal place as the uncertainty.

    Scalars and vectors

    A scalar has magnitude only (mass, speed, distance, energy, time, temperature). A vector has magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, weight).

    To add vectors, draw them tip to tail; the resultant goes from the start of the first to the end of the last. For two perpendicular vectors, use Pythagoras and trigonometry. To resolve a vector F at angle θ to the horizontal into two perpendicular components:

    Kinematics describes motion without asking what causes it. You need precise definitions, the meaning of the gradient and area of motion graphs, the four equations of uniformly accelerated motion, and projectile motion.

    Definitions

    Distance
    The total length of path travelled. A scalar.
    Displacement
    The distance in a straight line from the starting point, in a stated direction. A vector.
    Speed
    Distance travelled per unit time. A scalar.
    Velocity
    The rate of change of displacement. A vector.
    Acceleration
    The rate of change of velocity. A vector, in m s−2.

    Motion graphs

    • The gradient of a displacement–time graph is the velocity.
    • The gradient of a velocity–time graph is the acceleration.
    • The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement.

    Equations of uniformly accelerated motion

    These apply only when the acceleration is constant and the motion is in a straight line. Choose a positive direction and keep to it: a deceleration is a negative acceleration.

    Velocity after time tv = u + atLearn it
    Displacement from average velocitys = 12(u + v)tLearn it
    Displacement after time ts = ut + 12at2On formulae page
    Without timev2 = u2 + 2asOn formulae page

    Worked example

    A car accelerates uniformly from 12 m s−1 to 30 m s−1 in 6.0 s. Find its acceleration and the distance travelled.

    1. a = (v − u)/t = (30 − 12)/6.0 = 3.0 m s−2
    2. s = 12(u + v)t = 12(12 + 30) × 6.0 = 126 m

    a = 3.0 m s−2, s = 130 m (to 2 s.f.)

    Free fall and measuring g

    Near the Earth's surface, with no air resistance, every object falls with the same acceleration g = 9.81 m s−2.

    Experiment: drop a steel ball from rest through a measured height h, timing the fall with an electromagnet release and a trapdoor (or with light gates) connected to an electronic timer. Repeat for several heights. Since h = 12gt2, plot h against t2: it is a straight line through the origin with gradient g/2, so g = 2 × gradient. Using a graph also reveals a systematic delay in the release (an intercept).

    Projectile motion

    Without air resistance, the horizontal and vertical motions are independent:

    • Horizontally: no force, so constant velocity ucos θ. Use s = vt.
    • Vertically: constant acceleration g downwards, starting at usin θ. Use the equations of motion.
    • The time is the same for both, and links them.

    Worked example

    A ball is thrown horizontally at 15 m s−1 from the top of a cliff 20 m high. How far from the foot of the cliff does it land?

    1. Vertically: u = 0, s = 20 m, a = 9.81 m s−2, so 20 = 12(9.81)t2 and t = 2.02 s
    2. Horizontally: distance = 15 × 2.02 = 30.3 m

    The ball lands 30 m from the foot of the cliff.

    With air resistance, the projectile reaches a lower maximum height and a shorter range, and its path is no longer symmetrical: the descent is steeper than the ascent.

    Dynamics explains why motion changes. The central idea is momentum: a resultant force changes it, and in a collision it is conserved. Air resistance adds the idea of terminal velocity.

    Mass, weight and momentum

    Mass
    The property of an object that resists changes in its motion (its inertia). Measured in kg, the same everywhere.
    Weight
    The gravitational force on an object: W = mg. It depends on the gravitational field strength.
    Linear momentum
    The product of mass and velocity, p = mv. A vector, in kg m s−1 or N s.

    Newton's laws of motion

    1. First law: an object stays at rest or moves with constant velocity unless a resultant force acts on it.
    2. Second law: the resultant force on an object is proportional to its rate of change of momentum, and acts in the same direction: F = ΔpΔt. For constant mass this becomes F = ma.
    3. Third law: if body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. The two forces are of the same type and act on different bodies.
    Momentump = mvLearn it
    Newton's second lawF = ΔpΔtLearn it
    Constant massF = maLearn it
    WeightW = mgLearn it
    Third-law pairs

    The weight of a book on a table and the table's contact force on it are not a third-law pair: both act on the book. The pair to the book's weight is the book's gravitational pull on the Earth.

    Non-uniform motion: drag and terminal velocity

    Drag (air resistance or a viscous force in a liquid) acts opposite to the velocity and increases with speed. For an object falling from rest:

    1. At first the speed is zero, so there is no drag; the resultant force is the weight and the acceleration is g.
    2. As speed increases, drag increases, so the resultant force (weight − drag) and the acceleration decrease.
    3. Eventually drag equals weight. The resultant force is zero, so the object moves at a constant terminal velocity.

    Conservation of momentum

    Principle of conservation of momentum: for a system of interacting objects, the total momentum in any direction stays constant, provided no resultant external force acts on the system.

    • In every collision, momentum is conserved (and total energy is conserved).
    • In a perfectly elastic collision, kinetic energy is also conserved. In this case the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation.
    • In an inelastic collision, some kinetic energy becomes other forms (thermal energy, sound, deformation).
    • In two dimensions, momentum is conserved separately in each of two perpendicular directions.

    Worked example

    A trolley of mass 2.0 kg moving at 3.0 m s−1 hits a stationary trolley of mass 1.0 kg. They stick together. Find their common velocity and the kinetic energy lost.

    1. Momentum before = 2.0 × 3.0 = 6.0 kg m s−1 = momentum after = 3.0 v, so v = 2.0 m s−1
    2. KE before = ½ × 2.0 × 3.0² = 9.0 J; KE after = ½ × 3.0 × 2.0² = 6.0 J

    v = 2.0 m s−1; 3.0 J of kinetic energy is transferred to other forms, so the collision is inelastic.

    Forces can make things turn as well as accelerate. This topic covers moments and couples, the two conditions for equilibrium, and pressure in fluids, including why things float.

    Types of force

    • Weight: acts through the centre of gravity, the point where the whole weight of an object appears to act.
    • Normal contact (reaction) force: perpendicular to a surface.
    • Friction: acts along a surface, opposing relative motion.
    • Drag (viscous force, air resistance): opposes motion through a fluid and increases with speed.
    • Upthrust: the upward force a fluid exerts on an object in it.

    Turning effects

    Moment of a force
    The product of the force and the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. Unit: N m.
    Couple
    A pair of equal and opposite forces whose lines of action are parallel but do not coincide. A couple produces rotation only, with no resultant force.
    Torque of a couple
    The product of one of the forces and the perpendicular distance between their lines of action.
    Principle of moments
    For an object in equilibrium, the sum of the clockwise moments about any point equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that point.
    Moment (d = perpendicular distance to pivot)moment = F × dLearn it
    Torque of a couple (d = separation of the forces)torque = F × dLearn it

    Equilibrium

    An object is in equilibrium when both conditions hold:

    1. the resultant force is zero (in every direction), and
    2. the resultant moment (torque) about any point is zero.

    If three forces keep an object in equilibrium, they can be drawn tip to tail to form a closed triangle (a triangle of forces).

    Worked example

    A uniform plank 4.0 m long and of weight 200 N rests on supports at its ends A and B. A person of weight 600 N stands 1.0 m from A. Find the force from each support.

    1. Take moments about A (this removes the unknown force at A): RB × 4.0 = 600 × 1.0 + 200 × 2.0
    2. RB = 1000/4.0 = 250 N
    3. Vertical forces balance: RA = 600 + 200 − 250 = 550 N

    RA = 550 N, RB = 250 N

    Exam tipTake moments about the point where an unknown force acts. That force then has zero moment and drops out of the equation.

    Pressure is the normal force per unit area, in pascals (Pa = N m−2). In a fluid, pressure increases with depth: Δp = ρgΔh, where ρ is the density of the fluid. The total pressure at a depth also includes the atmospheric pressure on the surface.

    Upthrust arises because the pressure on the bottom surface of a submerged object is greater than on its top surface (the bottom is deeper). The resulting upward force equals the weight of fluid displaced, F = ρgV, where ρ is the fluid's density and V the volume submerged (Archimedes' principle). A floating object displaces its own weight of fluid.

    Energy is never created or destroyed, only transferred. Work is how a force transfers energy, power is how fast it happens, and efficiency tells you how much of it is useful.

    Work done

    Work done by a force is the product of the force and the displacement in the direction of the force. One joule is the work done when a force of 1 N moves its point of application 1 m in the direction of the force. If the force is at angle θ to the displacement, W = Fscos θ.

    A gas expanding against a constant external pressure p does work W = pΔV, where ΔV is the increase in volume.

    Energy conservation and efficiency

    Principle of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transferred from one form to another. The total energy of a closed system is constant.

    Work doneW = FsLearn it
    Work done by an expanding gasW = pΔVLearn it
    PowerP = WtLearn it
    Power at constant velocityP = FvLearn it
    Efficiencyefficiency = useful energy outputtotal energy input × 100%Learn it

    Power is the rate of doing work (or transferring energy), in watts: 1 W = 1 J s−1. When a force F moves an object at constant velocity v, P = Fs/t = Fv.

    Worked example

    A car's engine delivers a useful power of 30 kW while the car travels at a constant 25 m s−1. What is the total resistive force on the car?

    1. At constant velocity, driving force = resistive force
    2. F = P/v = 30 000/25 = 1200 N

    The resistive force is 1200 N.

    Gravitational potential energy is the energy an object has because of its position in a gravitational field. Kinetic energy is the energy it has because of its motion. You may be asked to derive both:

    • ΔEp = mgΔh: lifting a mass at constant speed needs a force equal to its weight, mg. The work done is force × distance = mgΔh, and this equals the gain in potential energy. It applies only in a uniform field, near the Earth's surface.
    • Ek = 12mv2: a constant force F accelerates a mass from rest through distance s. Work done W = Fs = mas. From v2 = u2 + 2as with u = 0, as = 12v2, so W = 12mv2, the kinetic energy gained.

    When forces stretch or squash a solid, it changes shape. Stress and strain describe this independently of the sample's size, and the Young modulus is the property of the material that links them.

    Hooke's law

    Tensile forces stretch an object; compressive forces squash it. Hooke's law: the extension of a spring (or wire) is proportional to the applied force, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.

    Hooke's law (k = spring constant)F = kxLearn it
    Stressσ = FALearn it
    Strainε = xLLearn it
    Young modulusE = σεLearn it

    Stress, strain and the Young modulus

    Stress
    The force per unit cross-sectional area, σ = F/A. Unit: Pa.
    Strain
    The extension per unit original length, ε = x/L. No unit.
    Young modulus
    Stress divided by strain, for a material obeying Hooke's law. Unit: Pa. A large Young modulus means a stiff material.

    Worked example

    A steel wire is 2.0 m long with diameter 0.50 mm. The Young modulus of steel is 2.0 × 1011 Pa. Find the extension produced by a load of 50 N.

    1. A = π(0.25 × 10−3)2 = 1.96 × 10−7 m2
    2. σ = 50/1.96 × 10−7 = 2.55 × 108 Pa
    3. ε = σ/E = 1.27 × 10−3, so x = εL = 2.5 × 10−3 m

    The extension is 2.5 mm.

    Measuring the Young modulus of a metal wire

    1. Use a long, thin wire (so the extension is measurable), clamped at one end and passing over a pulley, with a marker attached near the loaded end against a fixed millimetre scale. (Alternatively, use a vernier on a Searle's apparatus with a reference wire.)
    2. Measure the original length L from the clamp to the marker with a metre rule.
    3. Measure the diameter at several places with a micrometer screw gauge and take the mean; calculate A = πd2/4.
    4. Add masses in steps, recording the extension x for each load F. Check by removing the loads that the wire returns to its original length (it stayed elastic).
    5. Plot F against x. In the straight-line region, E = gradient × LA.

    Elastic and plastic behaviour

    Elastic deformation
    The object returns to its original shape and size when the load is removed.
    Plastic deformation
    The object is permanently deformed; it does not return to its original shape when the load is removed.
    Limit of proportionality
    The point beyond which force is no longer proportional to extension.
    Elastic limit
    The point beyond which the deformation becomes plastic.

    Elastic potential energy (strain energy) is the energy stored in a deformed object. It equals the area under the force–extension graph. In the Hooke's law region this area is a triangle: Ep = 12Fx = 12kx2.

    A progressive wave transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter. This topic covers the vocabulary of waves, the wave equation, the Doppler effect, the electromagnetic spectrum and polarisation.

    Describing a wave

    Displacement
    The distance of a point on the wave from its equilibrium position.
    Amplitude
    The maximum displacement from the equilibrium position.
    Wavelength
    The distance between two adjacent points on the wave that are in phase (such as two crests).
    Period
    The time for one complete oscillation of a point on the wave.
    Frequency
    The number of oscillations per unit time. Unit: hertz (Hz).
    Phase difference

    The distance travelled by the wave energy per unit time.

    The Doppler effect

    When a source of sound moves relative to a stationary observer, the observed frequency differs from the emitted frequency. As the source approaches, the wavefronts in front of it are squashed together (shorter wavelength), so the observer hears a higher frequency; as it recedes, the wavefronts are spread out and the frequency is lower.

    Doppler effect (source moving; − when approaching, + when receding)fo = fsvv ± vsOn formulae page

    Worked example

    2. Receding: fo = 700 × 340/(340 + 30) = 643 Hz

    When two waves meet, their displacements add. This principle of superposition explains stationary waves, interference fringes and the sharp bright lines of a diffraction grating.

    The principle of superposition

    When two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements at that point.

    Stationary waves

    A stationary (standing) wave forms when two progressive waves of the same frequency (and similar amplitude), travelling at the same speed in opposite directions, superpose. Usually one wave is a reflection of the other.

    • Nodes are points of zero amplitude; antinodes are points of maximum amplitude.
    • The distance between adjacent nodes (or adjacent antinodes) is λ/2.
    • All points between two adjacent nodes oscillate in phase; points either side of a node are in antiphase.
    • A stationary wave stores energy but does not transfer it.
    Progressive waveStationary wave
    EnergyTransferredNot transferred
    AmplitudeSame for all pointsVaries from zero at nodes to maximum at antinodes
    PhaseChanges continuously along the waveSame between adjacent nodes; reverses across a node

    Experiments

    • Stretched string: a string is driven by a vibration generator and passes over a pulley to a hanging mass. Adjust the frequency until a stationary wave forms. Measure the node-to-node distance with a metre rule, so λ = 2 × node separation and v = fλ.
    • Air column: a tuning fork of known frequency is held over a tube closed at one end (for example by water). Change the length of the air column until the sound is loudest (resonance). The first two resonant lengths differ by λ/2, so λ = 2(L2 − L1) and the speed of sound v = fλ. Using the difference removes the end correction.
    • Microwaves: a microwave transmitter faces a metal reflector. A detector moved along the line between them finds maxima and minima; adjacent minima (nodes) are λ/2 apart.

    Diffraction

    Diffraction is the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around the edge of an obstacle. It is most noticeable when the gap width is similar to the wavelength. The wavelength and speed do not change.

    Interference

    Two sources are coherent if they have a constant phase difference (which requires the same frequency). Interference fringes can only be observed with coherent sources. For two sources in phase:

    • Constructive interference (maximum) where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, nλ.
    • Destructive interference (minimum) where the path difference is (n + 12)λ.

    Two-source interference can be shown with water waves in a ripple tank, with microwaves from one transmitter passing through two slits, and with light through a double slit (Young's experiment).

    Double-slit fringe spacing (a = slit separation, D = slit-to-screen distance)λ = axDLearn it
    Diffraction grating (d = line spacing)dsin θ = nλLearn it

    Worked example

    In a double-slit experiment the slits are 0.50 mm apart and the screen is 1.20 m away. The fringe separation is 1.4 mm. Find the wavelength.

    1. λ = ax/D = (0.50 × 10−3)(1.4 × 10−3)/1.20

    λ = 5.8 × 10−7 m (580 nm)

    The diffraction grating

    A grating has a very large number of equally spaced parallel slits. Light diffracts through each slit, and the waves interfere constructively only at precise angles where the path difference between adjacent slits is a whole number of wavelengths, giving sharp, bright maxima. If the grating has N lines per metre, d = 1/N. The highest order seen is the largest integer n for which nλ/d ≤ 1.

    Electric current is a flow of charge, potential difference measures the energy each coulomb transfers, and resistance links the two. Learn the precise definitions: they are asked for again and again.

    Electric current and charge

Charge is quantised: any charge is a whole-number multiple of the elementary charge e = 1.60 × 10−19 C. One coulomb is the charge passing a point when a current of 1 A flows for 1 s.

    Potential difference and power

    The potential difference across a component is the energy transferred from electrical to other forms per unit charge passing through it. One volt is one joule per coulomb.

    Resistance and I–V characteristics

    Resistance is the potential difference across a component divided by the current through it. One ohm is one volt per ampere. Ohm's law: for a metallic conductor at constant temperature, the current is proportional to the potential difference.

    Why a filament's resistance rises: a larger current heats the metal, so its ions vibrate with greater amplitude and the electrons collide with them more often.

    • The resistance of a light-dependent resistor (LDR) decreases as light intensity increases.
    • The resistance of an NTC thermistor decreases as its temperature increases.

    Resistivity ρ is a property of the material: the resistance of a 1 m length with 1 m2 cross-section. Unit: Ω m. A wire's resistance increases with length and decreases with cross-sectional area.

    Potential difference

    2. V = IR = 2.0 × 5.5 = 11 V (lost volts = 1.0 V)

    Kirchhoff's laws

    First law
    The sum of the currents entering a junction equals the sum of the currents leaving it. This is a consequence of conservation of charge.
    Second law
    Around any closed loop in a circuit, the sum of the e.m.f.s equals the sum of the p.d.s. This is a consequence of conservation of energy.

    The series and parallel formulas follow from these laws. For series resistors the current is the same, so V = IR1 + IR2. For parallel resistors the p.d. is the same, so I = V/R1 + V/R2.

    Potential dividers

    Two resistors in series across a supply share its p.d. in the ratio of their resistances:

    Potential divider outputVout = R2R1 + R2 × VinLearn it

    Replacing one resistor with a thermistor or LDR makes a sensor: for example, with an LDR as R2, more light means a lower R2 and so a lower output p.d.

    The potentiometer

    A potentiometer is a uniform resistance wire with a steady current from a driver cell, so the p.d. along it is proportional to length. To compare two p.d.s, connect each in turn (through a galvanometer) to a sliding contact and find the balance (null) point where the galvanometer reads zero. Then V1/V2 = L1/L2. At balance no current is drawn from the cell being measured, so its e.m.f. is measured without any lost volts.

    An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, because its direction keeps changing. The acceleration, and the resultant force that causes it, point towards the centre.

    Angles and angular speed

    Radian
    The angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius. A full circle is 2π rad.
    Angular displacement
    The angle through which an object has moved around a circle, in radians.
    Angular speed ω
    The rate of change of angular displacement, in rad s−1.
    Angular speedω = 2πT = 2πfLearn it
    Linear and angular speedv = rωLearn it
    Centripetal accelerationa = rω2 = v2rLearn it

    Centripetal acceleration and force

    Velocity is a vector. Moving in a circle at constant speed, the direction of the velocity changes continuously, so the object is accelerating. This centripetal acceleration is directed towards the centre of the circle, perpendicular to the velocity, so it changes the direction but not the speed.

    By Newton's second law, a resultant force towards the centre is needed: the centripetal force. It is not a new kind of force; it is provided by an existing one:

    • a car on a flat bend: friction between the tyres and the road;
    • a satellite in orbit: gravitational force;
    • a ball whirled on a string: tension (plus a component of the weight in a vertical circle);
    • an electron orbiting in a magnetic field: the magnetic force.

    Because the force is perpendicular to the velocity, it does no work, and the kinetic energy stays constant.

    Worked example

    A car of mass 1200 kg travels round a flat bend of radius 50 m at 15 m s−1. What friction force is needed?

    1. F = mv2/r = 1200 × 152/50

    F = 5400 N, directed towards the centre of the bend.

    Vertical circles

    For a mass on a string in a vertical circle, at the top both tension and weight point to the centre: T + mg = mv2/r. At the bottom the tension points up and the weight down: T − mg = mv2/r. So the tension is greatest at the bottom, and the string is most likely to go slack at the top.

    Every mass attracts every other mass. This topic describes that attraction as a field, uses it to explain orbits, and introduces gravitational potential, which is always negative.

    The gravitational field

    A gravitational field is a region of space where a mass experiences a force. Gravitational field strength g at a point is the gravitational force per unit mass at that point, in N kg−1. Field lines show the direction of the force on a mass. Close to the Earth's surface the field is approximately uniform: parallel, equally spaced lines, with g = 9.81 N kg−1.

    Field strengthg = FmLearn it

    Newton's law of gravitation

    The gravitational force between two point masses is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation. For a uniform sphere, outside the sphere it acts as if all its mass were concentrated at its centre, so r is measured from the centre.

    Orbits

    For a satellite in a circular orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force:

    GMmr2 = mv2r, so v2 = GMr. Using v = 2πr/T gives T2 = 4π2GMr3, so T2 ∝ r3 (Kepler's third law).

    A geostationary satellite stays above the same point on the Earth. It must have a period of 24 hours, orbit above the equator, and move in the same direction as the Earth rotates (west to east). This fixes its orbital radius at about 4.2 × 107 m. Ground aerials do not need to track it.

    Gravitational potential

    The gravitational potential at a point is the work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to that point. It is zero at infinity and negative everywhere else, because gravity is attractive: work is done by the field as the mass moves in. The potential energy of a mass m at that point is Ep = mφ.

    When a mass moves from radius r1 to r2, the change in potential energy is ΔEp = GMm(1r1 − 1r2). Near the Earth's surface this reduces to mgΔh.

    The field strength is the negative of the potential gradient: g = −ΔφΔr.

    Temperature decides which way thermal energy flows. This topic covers the absolute temperature scale and how much energy it takes to warm a substance or change its state.

    Thermal equilibrium

    Thermal energy is transferred from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature. Objects in thermal equilibrium are at the same temperature, and there is no net transfer of thermal energy between them.

    Temperature scales

    A practical thermometer relies on a physical property that varies with temperature, such as the resistance of a thermistor, the e.m.f. of a thermocouple, or the expansion of a liquid. The thermodynamic (Kelvin) scale does not depend on the property of any particular substance. Its zero is absolute zero, the temperature at which a substance has minimum internal energy.

    Kelvin and CelsiusT/K = θ/°C + 273.15Learn it
    Specific heat capacityE = mcΔθLearn it
    Specific latent heatE = mLLearn it

    A temperature change of 1 K is the same as a change of 1 °C.

    Specific heat capacity and specific latent heat

    Specific heat capacity
    The energy needed per unit mass to raise the temperature of a substance by one degree (1 K). Unit: J kg−1 K−1.
    Specific latent heat
    The energy needed per unit mass to change the state of a substance without any change in temperature. Unit: J kg−1. Of fusion for melting, of vaporisation for boiling.

    During a change of state the temperature stays constant: the energy supplied increases the potential energy of the molecules (breaking bonds), not their kinetic energy. The specific latent heat of vaporisation is much larger than that of fusion because, on boiling, the molecules must be separated completely and work is done pushing back the atmosphere as the volume increases greatly.

    Measuring specific heat capacity: the electrical method

    1. Place an electrical heater and a thermometer in holes in a metal block (or in a liquid), and insulate it.
    2. Measure the mass with a balance. Record the starting temperature.
    3. Switch on the heater for a measured time t, recording the p.d. V and current I (or use a joulemeter).
    4. Record the highest temperature reached. Energy supplied = VIt = mcΔθ, so c = VItmΔθ.
    5. Energy losses to the surroundings make the value of c too high. To reduce them: insulate, or start below room temperature and finish the same amount above it.

    Worked example

    How long does a 50 W heater take to warm 0.50 kg of water from 20 °C to 30 °C? Water has c = 4200 J kg−1 K−1. Assume no energy is lost.

    1. E = mcΔθ = 0.50 × 4200 × 10 = 21 000 J
    2. t = E/P = 21 000/50

    t = 420 s (7.0 minutes)

    A gas is a huge number of molecules moving at random. The ideal gas equation describes its large-scale behaviour, and kinetic theory explains that behaviour from the motion of the molecules.

    The mole

    The mole is the SI unit of amount of substance. One mole contains NA = 6.02 × 1023 particles (the Avogadro constant). For n moles, the number of molecules is N = nNA.

    The equation of state

    An ideal gas is one that obeys pV ∝ T exactly, at all pressures, volumes and temperatures, where T is the thermodynamic temperature in kelvin. Real gases behave nearly ideally at low pressure and high temperature.

    Ideal gas equation (n moles)pV = nRTLearn it
    Ideal gas equation (N molecules)pV = NkTLearn it
    Kinetic theory pressurep = 13NmV⟨c2⟩On formulae page
    Mean kinetic energy of a molecule12m⟨c2⟩ = 32kTLearn it

    The Boltzmann constant is k = R/NA. The root-mean-square speed is crms = √⟨c2⟩.

    Kinetic theory

    Assumptions

    • A gas contains a very large number of molecules moving randomly.
    • The volume of the molecules is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
    • There are no forces between molecules except during collisions.
    • Collisions between molecules and with the walls are perfectly elastic.
    • The time spent in collisions is negligible compared with the time between collisions.

    Deriving the pressure

    1. A molecule of mass m moving with velocity component cx hits a wall and rebounds: its change of momentum is 2mcx.
    2. In a cube of side l, it returns to the same wall every 2l/cx, so the force on the wall is 2mcx2l/cx = mcx2l.
    3. For N molecules, total force = Nm⟨cx2⟩l, and pressure = forcel2 = Nm⟨cx2⟩V.
    4. Motion is random, so ⟨cx2⟩ = ⟨cy2⟩ = ⟨cz2⟩ = 13⟨c2⟩, giving pV = 13Nm⟨c2⟩.

    Linking to temperature: comparing pV = 13Nm⟨c2⟩ with pV = NkT gives 13m⟨c2⟩ = kT, so the mean kinetic energy of a molecule is 12m⟨c2⟩ = 32kT. The thermodynamic temperature is proportional to the mean kinetic energy of the molecules.

    Worked example

    2. ⟨c2⟩ = 3kT/m = 3 × 1.38 × 10−23 × 300/4.65 × 10−26 = 2.67 × 105 m2 s−2

    crms = 520 m s−1

    Internal energy is the total energy of the molecules in a system. The first law of thermodynamics is conservation of energy applied to it: heat it or do work on it, and its internal energy rises.

    Internal energy

    The internal energy of a system is the sum of a random distribution of the kinetic and potential energies of its molecules. A rise in temperature increases the kinetic energy of the molecules, so it increases the internal energy.

    In an ideal gas there are no intermolecular forces, so the molecules have no potential energy. The internal energy is purely kinetic, U = N × 32kT, and so it is proportional to the thermodynamic temperature.

    The first law of thermodynamics

    First lawΔU = q + WLearn it
    Work done on a gas at constant pressureW = −pΔVLearn it
    ΔU
    The increase in internal energy of the system.
    q
    The energy transferred to the system by heating (negative if the system loses thermal energy).
    W
    The work done on the system. When a gas is compressed, work is done on it and W is positive; when it expands, it does work on the surroundings and W is negative.
    Watch the signs

    9702 uses "work done on the system". Some textbooks use work done by the system, with the opposite sign. Always say in words which you mean.

    Process (ideal gas)What happens
    Constant temperature (isothermal)ΔU = 0, so q = −W. If the gas is compressed, the work done on it is lost as thermal energy.
    No heat transfer (rapid compression or expansion)q = 0, so ΔU = W. Rapid compression makes the gas hotter; rapid expansion makes it cooler.
    Constant volumeW = 0, so ΔU = q. All the heat supplied raises the internal energy.

    Worked example

    A gas absorbs 500 J of thermal energy and expands, doing 200 J of work on its surroundings. What is the change in its internal energy?

    1. q = +500 J; work done on the gas W = −200 J
    2. ΔU = 500 + (−200) = 300 J

    Damping and resonance

    Damping is the loss of energy from an oscillating system, usually by resistive forces, so the amplitude decreases.

    • Light damping: the system oscillates with amplitude decreasing gradually (exponentially) over many cycles; the period stays almost constant.
    • Critical damping: the system returns to equilibrium in the shortest possible time without oscillating (as in car suspension).
    • Heavy damping: the system returns to equilibrium very slowly, without oscillating.

    In forced oscillations, a periodic driving force makes a system oscillate at the driving frequency. Resonance is when the amplitude of the forced oscillation is a maximum, which happens when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the system. At resonance, energy is transferred most efficiently from the driver.

    Resonance can be useful (tuning a radio circuit, magnetic resonance imaging, musical instruments) or destructive (bridges and buildings driven by wind or earthquakes, rattling vehicle parts), which is why structures are damped.

    Magnetic fields push on moving charges and on currents, and changing magnetic flux induces an e.m.f. Together these explain motors, particle beams, Hall probes and generators.

    Magnetic fields

    A magnetic field is a region of space where a magnet, a current-carrying conductor or a moving charge experiences a force. Field lines run from north to south outside a magnet; closer lines mean a stronger field.

    Fields due to currents

    • Long straight wire: concentric circles around the wire, getting further apart with distance. Direction given by the right-hand grip rule (thumb along the current, fingers curl with the field).
    • Flat circular coil: like that of a short bar magnet, with field lines through the centre of the coil.
    • Long solenoid: a strong, uniform field inside, like a bar magnet outside. Inserting a ferrous (iron) core greatly increases the field strength.

    Two parallel wires carrying currents in the same direction attract; in opposite directions they repel. Each wire sits in the other's field.

    The direction of the force is given by Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current, thuMb = Motion (force). There is no force when the current is parallel to the field.

    Magnetic flux density B is the force per unit length per unit current on a conductor placed at right angles to the field. One tesla is the flux density when a wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field experiences a force of 1 N per metre of its length (1 T = 1 N A−1 m−1).

    Force on a moving charge

    A charge moving at right angles to a uniform magnetic field feels a force perpendicular to its velocity, so it moves in a circle: BQv = mv2r, giving r = mvBQ. Faster or heavier particles make bigger circles; a stronger field makes smaller ones. The force does no work, so the speed is constant.

    Velocity selection: crossed electric and magnetic fields exert opposite forces. Only particles with EQ = BQv, that is v = E/B, pass through undeflected.

    The Hall effect: when a current flows through a thin slice of conductor in a magnetic field perpendicular to it, the magnetic force pushes the charge carriers towards one side. Charge builds up until the electric force from it balances the magnetic force, producing a steady Hall voltage across the slice. Since VH ∝ B, a Hall probe measures flux density. Semiconductors give a larger Hall voltage than metals because their number density of charge carriers n is much smaller.

    Electromagnetic induction

    Magnetic flux
    The product of the flux density and the area perpendicular to the field, Φ = BA. Unit: weber (Wb).
    Magnetic flux linkage
    The product of the flux through a coil and its number of turns, NΦ.
    Faraday's law
    The induced e.m.f. is proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage.
    Lenz's law
    The direction of the induced e.m.f. is such that it tends to oppose the change producing it. This is a consequence of conservation of energy: work must be done to produce the induced current.

    An e.m.f. is induced whenever the flux linking a circuit changes: moving a magnet into a coil, moving a wire across a field, rotating a coil in a field, or changing the current in a nearby coil. Experiments show the e.m.f. is larger if the change is faster, the field stronger, or the coil has more turns.

    Light behaves as particles called photons, and electrons behave as waves. The photoelectric effect, electron diffraction and atomic line spectra are the three pieces of evidence you need to know.

    Photons

    Electromagnetic radiation has a particulate nature: it is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets of energy called photons. A photon's energy depends only on its frequency.

    Photon energyE = hf = hcλLearn it

    The electronvolt is the energy gained by an electron accelerated through a p.d. of 1 V: 1 eV = 1.60 × 10−19 J. A photon of wavelength 500 nm has energy hc/λ = 3.98 × 10−19 J = 2.49 eV.

    The photoelectric effect

    When electromagnetic radiation of high enough frequency falls on a metal surface, electrons are emitted. The observations:

    • No electrons are emitted below a threshold frequency, however intense the radiation.
    • Above it, emission is instantaneous, even for very weak radiation.
    • The maximum kinetic energy of the electrons depends on the frequency, not on the intensity.
    • The number of electrons emitted per second (the photoelectric current) is proportional to the intensity.

    Explanation: each electron absorbs the energy of one photon. It needs at least the work function Φ (the minimum energy needed to release an electron from the surface) to escape, so the photon energy hf must be at least Φ: the threshold frequency is f0 = Φ/h. Any extra energy becomes kinetic energy; electrons from deeper in the metal lose some on the way out, which is why the equation gives the maximum kinetic energy. Greater intensity means more photons per second, so more electrons, but not more energy per electron.

    The wave theory fails: a wave's energy depends on its intensity, so it predicts that intense light of any frequency would eventually release electrons, with energy increasing with intensity, and with a time delay at low intensity. None of these happen.

    Wave-particle duality

    Electron diffraction is evidence that particles have a wave nature. A beam of electrons passed through a thin film of polycrystalline graphite produces a pattern of concentric rings on a fluorescent screen, which is a diffraction pattern. Increasing the accelerating p.d. increases the electrons' momentum, so their wavelength decreases and the rings get smaller.

    Any particle of momentum p has a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p. For an electron accelerated from rest through p.d. V: 12mv2 = eV, so p = √2meV.

    Worked example

    Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through 100 V.

    1. p = √2 × 9.11 × 10−31 × 1.60 × 10−19 × 100 = 5.40 × 10−24 kg m s−1
    2. λ = h/p = 6.63 × 10−34/5.40 × 10−24

    λ = 1.2 × 10−10 m, similar to the spacing of atoms in a crystal, which is why diffraction is seen.

    Energy levels and line spectra

    Electrons in an isolated atom can only have certain discrete energy levels. When an electron falls from a higher level E1 to a lower level E2, a photon of energy hf = E1 − E2 is emitted. Because only certain differences are possible, only certain frequencies appear: an emission line spectrum of bright lines. When white light passes through a cool gas, photons with exactly these energies are absorbed to excite electrons, giving an absorption line spectrum: dark lines on a continuous spectrum.

    A nucleus has less mass than the protons and neutrons it is made from; the missing mass is the binding energy. This explains why fission and fusion release energy. The second half of the topic treats radioactive decay mathematically.

    Mass and energy

    Mass defect
    The difference between the total mass of the separate nucleons and the mass of the nucleus.
    Binding energy
    The minimum energy needed to separate a nucleus into its individual protons and neutrons. Binding energy = mass defect × c2.
    Binding energy per nucleon
    The binding energy divided by the nucleon number. A higher value means a more stable nucleus.

    Fission is the splitting of a large nucleus (such as uranium-235, after absorbing a neutron) into two smaller nuclei and a few neutrons. Fusion is the joining of two light nuclei (such as hydrogen isotopes) to form a heavier one; it needs very high temperatures to overcome the electrostatic repulsion. In both, the total mass of the products is less than the total mass before, and the mass difference appears as energy, ΔE = Δmc2.

    Worked example

    A helium-4 nucleus has mass 4.001506 u. A proton has mass 1.007276 u and a neutron 1.008665 u. Find the binding energy per nucleon in MeV. (1 u = 1.66 × 10−27 kg)

    1. Mass of separate nucleons = 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) = 4.031882 u
    2. Mass defect = 0.030376 u = 5.04 × 10−29 kg
    3. Binding energy = Δmc2 = 5.04 × 10−29 × (3.00 × 108)2 = 4.54 × 10−12 J = 28.4 MeV

    Binding energy per nucleon = 28.4/4 = 7.1 MeV

    Radioactive decay

    Radioactive decay is spontaneous (not affected by external factors such as temperature or pressure) and random (it is impossible to predict which nucleus will decay next, or when; each nucleus has a constant probability of decaying per unit time). Evidence for randomness: the count rate from a source fluctuates.

    Activity
    The number of nuclei decaying per unit time, in becquerels (1 Bq = 1 decay per second).
    Decay constant λ
    The probability that a nucleus decays per unit time.
    Half-life
    The mean time taken for half of the active nuclei in a sample to decay.

    Because A = λN, the number of nuclei, the activity and the count rate all decay exponentially with the same half-life. After n half-lives, the fraction remaining is (12)n. Plotting ln A against t gives a straight line of gradient −λ.

    Three ways of seeing inside the body: ultrasound reflects from boundaries between tissues, X-rays are absorbed differently by bone and soft tissue, and PET detects gamma photons from a radioactive tracer.

    Ultrasound

    Ultrasound is sound with a frequency above the range of human hearing (above 20 kHz); medical scans use about 1 to 10 MHz.

    The piezoelectric transducer

    • Generating: an alternating p.d. applied across a piezoelectric crystal makes it change shape (expand and contract) at the same frequency, so it vibrates and emits ultrasound. The crystal's thickness is chosen so it resonates at the required frequency.
    • Detecting: when reflected ultrasound hits the crystal, it is made to vibrate, and the changing shape produces an alternating p.d. across it, which is processed.

    Reflection at boundaries

    The specific acoustic impedance Z of a medium is its density × the speed of sound in it, unit kg m−2 s−1. When ultrasound meets a boundary, the fraction of intensity reflected depends on the difference in Z. A large difference means strong reflection.

    Why gel is used: air and skin have very different impedances, so almost all ultrasound would be reflected at the skin. A gel with an impedance close to skin's fills the gap and removes the air layer, so most of the ultrasound enters the body.

    In an A-scan, the time between sending a pulse and receiving each echo gives the depth of each boundary: depth = 12ct. A B-scan combines many A-scans taken at different angles into a two-dimensional image.

    X-rays

    In an X-ray tube, electrons emitted from a heated cathode are accelerated through a large p.d. (tens of kilovolts) and strike a metal target (such as tungsten). Most of their energy becomes thermal energy, so the anode is cooled, often by rotating it. About 1% becomes X-rays:

    • Braking radiation: electrons decelerating in the target emit a continuous spectrum of X-ray photons. The highest-energy photon carries all of one electron's kinetic energy eV, which sets a minimum wavelength λmin = hc/eV.
    • Characteristic lines: sharp peaks at wavelengths set by electron energy levels in the target atoms.

    A higher accelerating p.d. gives "harder" X-rays (higher photon energies, more penetrating). Increasing the tube current gives more photons, so a more intense beam, but does not change the photon energies.

    Image quality. Sharpness improves with a smaller target area (a near point source) and with a lead grid that stops scattered X-rays. Contrast depends on the difference in attenuation between tissues: bone attenuates far more than soft tissue. Contrast media such as barium (high attenuation) are used to show soft tissues such as the digestive tract.

    CT scanning: an X-ray tube and detectors rotate around the patient, taking images of a thin slice from many angles. A computer combines them to find the attenuation in each small volume element (voxel) of the slice. Repeating for many slices builds a three-dimensional image that can be viewed from any angle.

    PET scanning

    1. A tracer is injected: a substance used by the body (such as a glucose analogue, FDG) labelled with a β+-emitting isotope such as fluorine-18, with a short half-life.
    2. The tracer collects where it is used most, for example in active tumours.
    3. Each emitted positron travels a very short distance before meeting an electron. They annihilate, turning their mass into two gamma photons.
    4. To conserve momentum, the two photons travel in opposite directions, each with energy mec2 = 0.51 MeV (8.2 × 10−14 J).
    5. A ring of detectors around the patient detects both photons. The line between the two detectors, and the small difference in arrival times, locate where the annihilation happened. Many events build up an image of tracer concentration.

    How do we measure the size of a star we can't resolve, or the distance to a galaxy? Luminosity, the inverse-square law, the laws of black-body radiation and redshift together answer both questions, and point to an expanding universe.

    Luminosity and standard candles

    Luminosity
    The total power of the electromagnetic radiation emitted by a star, in watts.
    Radiant flux intensity
    The radiant power passing normally through unit area, in W m−2. This is what we measure from Earth.
    Standard candle
    An astronomical object of known luminosity. Examples: Cepheid variable stars (whose period of brightness variation is linked to their luminosity) and type 1a supernovae (which all reach about the same peak luminosity).

    Radiation spreads out equally in all directions over the surface of a sphere of area 4πd2. If we know a standard candle's luminosity and measure its flux, we can find its distance: d = √L/4πF.

    Stellar radii

    A star behaves approximately as a black body. Wien's displacement law: the wavelength at which the intensity of radiation is greatest is inversely proportional to the thermodynamic temperature, λmaxT = constant (about 2.9 × 10−3 m K). So a star's colour gives its surface temperature: hotter stars are bluer.

    The Stefan–Boltzmann law links luminosity to surface temperature and radius. Knowing L (from a standard candle or flux and distance) and T (from Wien's law), the radius is r = √L4πσT4.

    Worked example

    The Sun's luminosity is 3.83 × 1026 W and its spectrum peaks at about 500 nm. Estimate its surface temperature and radius. (Wien's constant = 2.9 × 10−3 m K)

    1. T = 2.9 × 10−3/500 × 10−9 = 5800 K
    2. r = √3.83 × 10264π × 5.67 × 10−8 × 58004

    r = 6.9 × 108 m

    Hubble's law and the Big Bang

    Spectral lines in light from distant galaxies are shifted to longer wavelengths: a redshift. This is the Doppler effect for light from a source moving away. The fractional shift gives the recession speed: v ≈ cΔλ/λ (for speeds much less than c).

    Hubble's law: the speed at which a galaxy is receding is proportional to its distance from us, v ≈ H0d, where H0 is the Hubble constant (about 2.2 × 10−18 s−1).

    Since more distant galaxies recede faster, and in every direction, the universe is expanding. Running the expansion backwards suggests it began from an extremely small, hot, dense state: the Big Bang. A rough age for the universe is 1/H0.
